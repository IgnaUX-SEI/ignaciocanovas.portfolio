The Thesis

"Users didn't distrust the AI.
They distrusted the interface around it."

This finding showed up in a search bar, a results page, and a browser extension. Three different products. Three different teams. One researcher. And on every surface, the same pattern: the AI capability was strong. The path to it was broken. Users who reached the AI output reacted positively — almost without exception. The interface was the problem, not the intelligence behind it.

This case study is about what happens when trust in an AI product is not determined by the AI itself — but by the visual feedback, the state indicators, the latency communication, and the conversational affordances that surround it.

The Setup

Two types of searchers. One extension. Same friction everywhere.

Two types of browser users encounter AI in search differently. The NTP user opens from the homepage — the most iconic search interface in the world. The Omnibox user goes straight to the URL bar, bypassing the homepage entirely. Both land on the same search results page with the same AI features. And both, in parallel, interacted with a browser extension that let them build their own AI tools from scratch.

Surface 01 · NTP
Homepage users
Familiar at first. Confidence built by recognition. Dissonance grew day by day as the familiar interface revealed how much had changed.
Surface 02 · Omnibox
URL-bar-first users
Task-first arrival. No homepage familiarity. The AI button was smaller, more peripheral, easier to miss before any expectation had formed.
Surface 03 · Extension
Builder users
A side panel for building reusable AI tools. The premise: no prompt engineering required. The reality: it was the unacknowledged prerequisite for everything.
"Both NTP and Omnibox users landed on the same SRP. Different entry points. The same destination. And the same trust failures waiting for them there."

01 · AIM Button

The toggle problem is a trust problem.

Most users typed their query and hit Enter before scanning for new interface elements. The AIM button wasn't hidden — it was outside the field of attention at the moment that mattered. Discovery was accidental. Once found, adoption was fast. The problem that persisted: users never knew if AI Mode was on or off. Some clicked three times, cycling through states without knowing which they were in. Without state clarity, users couldn't trust their own actions — which meant they couldn't trust the output, even when it was good.

01
Missed by habit, not by design
Twenty years of muscle memory — type and hit Enter — meant users were already on the SRP before scanning for new elements. Both NTP and Omnibox users missed the button equally. The entry point didn't change the outcome.
02
No activation feedback
Clicking AIM produced no clear visual signal. No color change. No state indicator. Users clicked multiple times unsure if anything had registered.
AIM
No feedback. No state.
02 · AI Fulfillment on the SRP

They found the AI. Then they couldn't continue.

When users reached AI fulfillment, the reaction was almost always positive. Conversational answers felt different from search results. The sourcing sidebar — once found — was strongly valued. Follow-up questions felt like a real dialogue. But the moment users wanted to continue or refine, the interface stopped them. The search field at the top was read-only in AI Mode. The chat input was buried below everything. Twenty years of muscle memory said type at the top. The interface required scrolling to the bottom. Same problem on the extension: after submission, the input went grey. The conversation was structurally over. Users wandered the output clicking everywhere, looking for a way to reply. There wasn't one.

03 · Action Chips & Latency

They didn't know if it was AI Mode, Deep Search, or broken. They just had to wait.

Once users were inside the AI experience, the platform had multiple ways to send them into a long, undisclosed wait — and no way to communicate what was happening or let them out. Deep Dive Search inside AIO. Deep Search inside AIM. Research a Topic. All three could trigger 4 to 5 minute waits with no warning, no progress indicator, and no way to stop. Users couldn't distinguish between "thinking" and "broken." Many left and never returned to those features.

00
Two paths into a heavy layer — no warning for either
Deep Dive Search inside AIO and Deep Search inside AIM could both trigger 4–5 minute waits. A user who never activated AI Mode could still end up in a heavy processing layer by clicking the wrong thing. No cue, no estimate, no way to stop. "Thinking..." communicated busy — nothing else. Many concluded the system had broken. Trust slipped not because the AI failed to deliver, but because it failed to communicate.
01
Research a Topic — the most praised interaction and the most abandoned feature
Clarifying questions before processing earned immediate praise — users felt genuinely understood. Then the wait began. 4 to 5 minutes. No progress indicator. No way to stop. Users felt trapped. Most opened a new tab and left. They never came back to it. The clarifying questions earned trust. The undisclosed latency destroyed it. One feature. One session. Two completely opposite emotional moments.
02
Ask About a Tab — concept valued, execution limited
Contextual summaries from the current tab saved time and felt genuinely useful. But it only referenced the last tab — users had to reorganize their tabs to use it on others. The core purpose was also confusing — users didn't know if it would summarize, explain, or reference the page.
03
Create an Image — excited, then confused
The concept was immediately recognized and valued. Execution was broken: users expected a generated image and received search results. The activation loop compounded the problem — clicking again unknowingly deactivated the feature entirely.
04
Suggested Questions — felt native
When contextually aware — asking about departure dates, budget, preferences — they felt like genuine personalization. Familiar enough to feel native. Useful enough to change how users searched.
"The design of the wait matters as much as the wait itself."
04 · The Extension

A browser extension that asked users to become prompt engineers.

SKILLS was a proof-of-concept AI extension — a side panel where users could build reusable AI tools by writing prompts, without needing to know how to prompt. That tension was the study. Users arrived with real problems: allergy checking, medical research, recipe conversion, pilot exam study tools. Not abstract testing. Real life. The system failed many of them immediately — and earned fierce loyalty from those it didn't.

Create New panel — prompt field, pre-built library, preview area.
Browser Skills panel — skill cards and the Create new skill entry point.
Day 0
Understood immediately. Broken immediately.
Skills failed to run on specific pages. Output codes were undefined. A bug deleted text mid-entry. Latency killed trust before it had a chance to form. And yet — when it worked: "This is a hundred percent something I would use."
Day 1
The prompting divide.
7 of 16 users found skill creation easy. 7 found it difficult. A perfect split. Users with domain knowledge unlocked real value. Users without it hit a wall the interface provided no bridge over. Prompting literacy was the unacknowledged prerequisite.
Day 2
The invisible feature nobody found.
The Magic Wand — a prompt refinement tool — sat quietly in the input field. Only 2 of 16 users found it. The other 14: "I did not know it existed." The feature worked well. It was just invisible. Discoverability is a design problem, not a user problem.
Day 3
Vision expansion.
By day 3, users stopped asking for bug fixes and started imagining futures — AR holographic content, adaptive skills that evolve in real time, AI preflight safety systems. The concept earned loyalty the product hadn't yet deserved.

05 · Three Mindsets

Not demographics. Relationship to uncertainty.

Across all three surfaces, users sorted themselves not by age, technical skill, or task complexity — but by how they responded when AI failed to meet their expectation. Three archetypes emerged most clearly in the extension, where the builder dynamic made behavioral patterns visible over four days. But the same patterns appeared in how users engaged with AIM, chips, and the SRP. These are not SKILLS user types. They are universal AI interaction archetypes.

01
The Builder
Wants to customize and extend. Sees AI as a programmable platform. Pushes limits and pivots when blocked — never gives up. In search: manipulated chips and AIM layout. In the extension: built domain-specific tools immediately. The system was always too limited for them. They kept building anyway. Verdict: "Cool, but too limited."
02
The Optimizer
Motivated by time savings. Adopts fast when it works — early wins build momentum. Any friction immediately breaks the efficiency promise. In search: abandoned Research a Topic the moment latency hit. In the extension: the duplicate skill bug added cleanup work that contradicted their reason for being there. Verdict: "Not dependable enough yet."
03
The Verifier
Needs trustworthy, accurate, traceable output above all else. One factual error ends the relationship — there is no recovery path for this mindset. In search: wouldn't act on AI output they couldn't verify. In the extension: a single undefined output code triggered complete confidence collapse. Unlike the Builder who pivots, the Verifier leaves. Verdict: "I would never trust it."
"Design for the Verifier first. The Builder will find workarounds. The Optimizer will return when it's faster. The Verifier will not come back."
What It All Means

Trust is the product. Not the AI — the interface that gets users to it.

Every surface in this research program had the same core problem: strong AI capability surrounded by an interface that couldn't communicate its own state, couldn't guide users to the right inputs, and couldn't hold a conversation once one had started. The AI earned trust every time it was reached. The path to it did not.

01
State visibility is a trust system
Every unread system state is a trust withdrawal. AIM on or off? Submission registered or not? Output reliable or hallucinated? When users can't read the system, they assume it's broken — even when it isn't.
02
Conversational expectation is universal
Users brought conversational expectations to every surface — search bars, AI results pages, prompt-based tools. Every system required procedurally different behavior. None of them communicated that difference.
03
Discoverability is a design problem, not a user problem
The Magic Wand worked well for 2 of 16 users. The AIM button worked well for everyone who found it. Features weren't missing — they were invisible. Invisible is a different problem with a different solution.
04
Prompting literacy cannot be a prerequisite
Both products positioned themselves as accessible to everyday users. Both required prompting strategies users were never taught. The design challenge is not teaching users to prompt — it is building systems that guide them without requiring them to know they need guidance.
05
The wishlist is the roadmap
By day 3, extension users stopped requesting fixes and started imagining futures — adaptive skills, multi-tab synthesis, AR integration. The research documented the product three years ahead of where it was. That gap is the most honest product roadmap a research team can produce.
"Users didn't fail to adopt AI. They failed to find it — and when they found it, the interface failed to tell them what it was doing."

Design set up to fail from the start.

A major fintech platform serving credit unions, formed through the merger of two legacy companies. Post-merger integration was underway — but design was structurally invisible. Business decisions were made before design was ever in the room.

Success was measured by ticket throughput, not experience outcomes. Seven weeks to diagnose a systemic problem and build a future-state operating model that could actually scale.

Data Mise en Place

A framework for traceable AI synthesis in qualitative research — so findings can be defended, not just delivered.

Abstract

The quality of AI synthesis is not determined by the model alone.

This paper proposes a structured framework for AI-assisted qualitative synthesis that maintains the traceability and rigor of grounded theory methodology while operating at the speed and scale that modern research practice demands.

The framework addresses a gap that current AI tools do not solve: the absence of a verifiable chain of custody from raw participant data to synthesized insight. It was developed from practice between 2022 and 2026.

The central argument is twofold. First, the quality of AI synthesis depends primarily on the structure of the data entering the system. Second, human presence is not optional in AI-assisted research — it is a foundational requirement. Without a researcher who has witnessed the data, there is no basis for validating what the AI produces.

"If you cannot trace it back to the source, it does not belong on the plate." — The mise en place principle, applied to research.
The Problem

AI tools appear trustworthy regardless of input quality.

AI language models are designed — at the interface level — to appear trustworthy. They deliver outputs with confidence and fluency regardless of the quality of the input. When researchers feed unstructured, noisy, or poorly organized data into these systems, the output can appear rigorous while being partially or wholly fabricated.

This is not a failure of the technology. It is a failure of the methodology surrounding it. Most practitioners have responded in one of two ways: accepting AI output without verification, or rejecting AI assistance entirely. Both responses are inadequate.

A third path exists: designing the data structure and human presence that makes AI synthesis verifiable. This is what the framework proposes.

The insight that drives the framework
AI tools constrained to a well-defined input produce more reliable output than AI tools given unconstrained access to raw data. The framework applies constraint through data architecture and structured prompting — making any AI tool more defensible.
Theoretical Grounding

Built from practice. Validated by theory.

This framework was developed independently through practice before its connections to existing methodology were recognized. In January 2026, the author encountered grounded theory methodology (Strauss & Corbin, 1998) for the first time and found that the pipeline built through trial and error over three years was structurally identical to what Strauss and Corbin had formalized decades earlier.

The parallel is significant. Grounded theory was developed precisely because researchers recognized that imposing analytical frameworks on qualitative data before the data had been examined produced findings that reflected the researcher's assumptions rather than participants' actual experiences. The same risk applies to AI-assisted synthesis.

The framework adapts principles from grounded theory for large language model-assisted synthesis — including bottom-up coding from data, researcher immersion, constant comparison, and data saturation.

Core Concept

An atomic observation is one value. One piece of data.

The unit of analysis in this framework is not the sentence. It is the value-bearing claim. A sentence can contain one value or many. The researcher's job — and the AI's job under constraint — is to decompose meaning until each unit is irreducible.

The decision rule: Can this observation be split further without losing a distinct piece of meaning? If yes — split it.

One observation = one value = one evidence quote = one evidence type.

Common observation types that emerge across research contexts: Preference, Emotional state, Behavioral pattern, Causal relationship, Contradiction, Social validation, Expectation. This is not a fixed taxonomy — different research contexts will surface different types.

The backlog principle
When a statement cannot be cleanly decomposed, it is not discarded — it is preserved with "ambiguity" as its evidence type. If the same unresolved signal appears across multiple participants, the pattern itself becomes attributable. The framework is not anti-ambiguity. It is pro-defensibility.
Path A — Generative Research

The data reveals the themes. You don't impose them.

For generative research (interviews, exploratory sessions), the pipeline follows an inductive path. Codes emerge bottom-up from atomic observations. The data generates the code.

Structured Transcript → Clean Notes → Anonymization → Atomic Observations → Evidence Type → Inductive Codes → Axial Codes → Themes
01
Structured Transcript
Raw transcript + observation notes combined. Speaker labels added, metadata included, observation notes embedded at the exact moments they occurred.
02
Clean Notes / Reconciliation
AI removes filler, normalizes statements, replaces facilitator questions with neutral context lines. Researcher observation notes stay unchanged.
03
Anonymization
All names and sensitive content replaced based on pre-defined anonymization notes. Nothing disappears silently — redactions are flagged.
04
Atomic Observations
Each piece of clean data broken into atomic observations — one idea, one source, no interpretations. Each receives a unique code that travels unchanged through every subsequent stage.
05
Evidence Type / Taxonomy
Applied after all observations exist — never during extraction. The AI classifies against a taxonomy the researcher designed before the pipeline ran.
06
Inductive → Axial → Themes
Codes emerge bottom-up. Inductive codes cluster into axial codes — higher-order patterns visible across participants. Axial codes cluster into themes that name what the data has revealed.
Path B — Evaluative Research

The data measures against a known standard.

For evaluative research (user testing), deductive clusters are defined by the research design before the pipeline runs. The UX Area is the deductive code — brought in from outside, not discovered from the data.

Structured Transcript → Clean Notes → Anonymization → Atomic Observations → Evidence Type → UX Area → Cluster Description → Findings → Recommendations
The critical reconciliation step
In user testing, data cleaning is a genuine analytical task — not housekeeping. A participant who says "this is clear" while clicking the wrong button is telling us something important with their behavior that their words are not. The researcher makes that judgment call. The reconciled version becomes the clean note. The AI works from this truth.
What It Produces

A structured, queryable knowledge asset — not a deck.

The output of this pipeline — whether themes or findings — is defensible, filterable, and honest.

✓
Defensible
Every conclusion traces to its source. No finding exists without attributed evidence. The chain from recommendation to participant quote is unbroken.
✓
Filterable
Because observations are structured in a spreadsheet with coded columns, the data can be filtered by participant, session, topic, evidence type, UX area, or business risk.
✓
Honest
Because the pipeline requires human presence at every validation gate, the output reflects what participants actually said and did — not what the model inferred they probably meant.
"Traceability creates auditability, not epistemic validity. What the pipeline guarantees is not that the output is correct — it is that the output is checkable."
Implications

The researcher role is not diminished. It is elevated.

Critical thinking moves upstream — into data architecture decisions — and downstream — into validation against lived experience. The researcher who develops these skills becomes more strategically valuable. The researcher who does not risks producing synthesis that looks rigorous and cannot be defended.

On hallucinations: this pipeline does not eliminate them. No methodology does. What it changes is detectability. Errors that previously passed unnoticed become visible at the validation gates, because the structured chain of custody gives the researcher something concrete to verify.

The pipeline does not prevent AI from making mistakes. It makes mistakes findable. That is the real reliability guarantee.
